# Big Builds — Disaster Reference List (Aug 15, 2026)

~50 real, verified events across ships / trains / buildings, picked for
having an actual engineering story (a cause, a lesson, an improvement) —
not just picked for scale of loss. Flags below tell you how much care an
entry needs before it becomes a script.

**Flags:**
- 🟢 **Light** — minimal/no casualties, or enough distance/mystery that
  script.js's existing tone rules handle it easily
- 🟡 **Moderate** — real loss of life, but a clear redemptive engineering
  angle (a reform, a lesson, a save) to build the episode around
- 🟠 **Handle gently** — heavier event; script.js needs to lean hard on
  its "spend most of the script on ambition/ingenuity" rule, and these
  are good candidates to space out rather than run back-to-back

Facts below are drawn from general historical sources — spot-check
specific numbers before they ship in a real episode, same standard the
rest of this project holds for anything feeding a kid-facing script.

## Adjacent category, if he's open to it: dams

Not one of your three categories, but if trains/ships/buildings run dry
before events.js needs refreshing, dam failures are the same genre of
story (a design flaw + a lesson):
- **St. Francis Dam** (1928, California) — site/design flaw, directly
  created California's dam safety program
- **South Fork Dam / Johnstown Flood** (1889, Pennsylvania) — poorly
  maintained dam, one of the first disasters with major organized relief
  response (early American Red Cross)

## Why the list stops around 50, not 100

Most "deadliest ever" lists lean heavily on wartime sinkings, terrorism,
and a handful of incidents where I wasn't confident enough in the
specific facts to put them in a script a kid hears as true. Rather than
pad the count with either unverified details or events that need to be
sanitized past the point of being honest, this list stops at real,
well-documented, actually-tellable stories. Happy to keep expanding it —
loosening to bridges, dams, and aviation/space (which the "Big Builds"
format handles fine) would get you to 100 faster than staying strict to
three categories.

## 🌟 Engineering Marvels & Stories of Human Ingenuity

The original list is strongest when a failure reveals a lesson. This appendix deliberately adds the
other half of the show's emotional range: stories where people imagined something that did not
exist, solved a seemingly impossible problem, and built it.

The recurring narrative engine is:

**Impossible problem → curious people → experiments → setbacks → breakthrough → what we learned**

**Source note:** These entries were expanded using outside historical/engineering context. As with
the disaster list, spot-check specific numbers, dates, and superlatives before they become
kid-facing scripts.

| Marvel | Era | Location | Story hook | Lesson / human idea | Flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Golden Gate Bridge | 1933–1937 | San Francisco, USA | Engineers proposed spanning a windy, foggy, earthquake-prone strait that many considered impractical. | Brooklyn Bridge | 1869–1883 | New York, USA | John and Washington Roebling's bridge combined enormous suspension cables, underwater foundations, and a record-breaking span. | A vision may take decades and generations of people to finish. | 🟢 |

| Millau Viaduct | 2001–2004 | France | One of the world's tallest bridge structures carries a road across a deep valley with remarkably slender towers. | Strength does not always have to look heavy; good design can be both elegant and powerful. | 🟢 |
| Akashi Kaikyō Bridge | 1988–1998 | Japan | A suspension bridge was built across a difficult strait with powerful winds, currents, and earthquakes. | Engineers design for forces they cannot see and conditions they cannot control. | 🟢 |

## 🎙️ Strongest "Keeper of Wonders" Episodes

For the narrator persona, these are the strongest first choices:

1. **The Golden Gate Bridge — "The Bridge They Said Couldn't Be Built"**
2. **The Wright Brothers — "Two Bicycle Makers Try to Fly"**
3. **The Panama Canal — "The Day We Moved Ships Over a Mountain"**
4. **Apollo 11 — "How We Taught a Machine to Find the Moon"**
5. **Hubble — "The Telescope That Got a Second Chance"**
6. **James Webb — "How Do You Fold a Telescope Bigger Than a House?"**
7. **Hoover Dam — "How Do You Build a Mountain-Sized Wall?"**
8. **The Channel Tunnel — "The People Who Dug Toward Each Other"**
9. **Roman Aqueducts — "How Did They Make Water Run Uphill?"**
10. **International Space Station — "We Built a House in Space"**

These have an especially useful property for a 2nd–3rd grade audience: **the child can understand
the problem before understanding the engineering.**

That gives the narrator a natural opening:

> **"Now, I want you to imagine something..."**

Then the child discovers the engineering solution alongside him.

## Suggested balance for the overall project

The existing disaster list is excellent for **"we learned because something went wrong."**
The new material gives the project its other emotional register:

**We dreamed → we struggled → we figured it out → now the impossible is ordinary.**

A healthy episode mix might therefore be roughly:

- **40% — Marvels:** "Look what we built."
- **25% — Inventors:** "Look how someone figured it out."
- **20% — Lessons from failure:** "Look what went wrong, and what we changed."
- **15% — Systems/collaboration:** "Look what happens when thousands of people work together."

That keeps the project from becoming a catalog of disasters and makes the larger theme **human
ingenuity, curiosity, humility, and perseverance**.
